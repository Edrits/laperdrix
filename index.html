Words by Peter Kelly

Every few years, we return to La Perdrix, an old converted monastery in the tiny French village of Saint-Martin-de-Ribérac. We've been coming here for decades, to the point that arriving no longer really feels like the beginning of a holiday. It feels like coming home.

La Perdrix is a beautiful, sprawling, slightly ramshackle place, the sort of house that seems to have accumulated rather than been decorated. Its rooms are filled with paintings, books, pottery, strange objects, old furniture and countless knick-knacks, each apparently with a story behind it. There is an extraordinary collection of hats, which inevitably migrate onto people's heads as evenings progress, and around almost every corner there is something peculiar or beautiful you could swear you had never noticed on the previous visit.

The building began life as a monastery in the thirteenth century, and something of that history remains in its thick walls, enormous rooms and wonderfully irregular layout. There are bedrooms seemingly everywhere, along with two swimming pools, rambling gardens and a huge art-studio space. From the house you look across towards the old village church, a view which makes the whole place feel suspended somewhere between the everyday and a much older France.

At its heart is the enormous dining table, big enough for around twenty-five people, sitting outside beneath a pergola engulfed in grapevines. This is where breakfasts drift into late mornings, where bottles and plates multiply over long lunches, and where everyone eventually reconvenes in the evening for the daily feast.

Johnny and Ingrid have lived at La Perdrix since 2007, and the place bears the imprint of the life they have made there: creative, eccentric, generous and gloriously unpolished. Nothing feels over-designed or precious. Things lean, creak, grow wild and occasionally require improvisation. That is part of its charm.

For us, though, La Perdrix is more than a beautiful house in France. It is a repository of family history. Every return folds another holiday into all the ones that came before it: different generations around the same table, familiar rooms redistributed among us, old jokes revived, new memories quietly attaching themselves to old ones. After so many years, it is difficult to separate the place itself from the people we have been there.

La Perdrix is our home away from home, only larger, stranger, sunnier, and with considerably more hats.
